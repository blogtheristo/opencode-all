What the US and the EU can learn from Brexit — and why interdependence is the moat
for the Coalition of the Willing.

30 Jun 2026 · by Risto Anton Paarni

The fact, in one line

Ten years after the referendum, mainstream estimates put the long-run cost of Brexit at
roughly 4 to 6 percent of UK GDP. The sovereignty goal was popular. The operational bill
was steep.

Brexit asked a fair question: who decides?

A nation wanting to control its own laws, borders, and money is not a character flaw. It
is the oldest question in politics. The instinct behind Leave — that distant
rule-making had drifted past the consent of the ruled — was not imaginary. Plenty of
people in Helsinki, Paris, and Washington feel the same pull.

And because the question was right, the answer deserves an honest look at what it cost.

## The goal was partly right

Sovereignty is real value. A country that cannot set its own rules cannot answer to its
own voters. The EU itself now uses the same logic — data residency, the Cloud and AI
Development Act, sovereign compute — to keep critical workloads under European law.
That is sovereignty as strategy, and it is sound.

So the disagreement with Brexit is not about the goal. It is about the price someone paid
to reach it, and whether a cheaper road was open.

## And then it paid retail

A hard exit bought sovereignty at the most expensive counter in the shop. New customs
friction. Thinner trade in goods and services. Fewer workers in the sectors that needed
them. Investment that waited, then went elsewhere.

None of that shows up on a single day. It shows up as a slow drag — a few tenths of
a percent a year, compounding. Run that for a decade and the estimates land in the same
neighbourhood: a permanent output gap of several percent. Sovereignty arrived. So did the
invoice.

And here is the part the 6 percent headline hides. The cost was asymmetric. The pain came
immediate and concentrated — trade friction at the border, talent walking out the
door, regulatory work done twice. The benefit was diffuse and theoretical: autonomy you
could point to on paper but rarely spend.

That asymmetry is the whole lesson. Sovereignty without interdependence has a negative
return: you pay now, in full, for an upside that stays a promise. Sovereignty
with interdependence compounds the other way — you keep the trade, keep the
talent, and still own the rules that matter.

The honest part about the number

"6 percent of GDP" is an estimate, not a meter reading. The UK's own Office for
Budget Responsibility models a long-run productivity hit of about 4 percent; several
independent analyses put the cumulative loss closer to 5 or 6. No one can run the
counterfactual where Britain stayed, so treat the figure as a range with a clear
direction, not a settled fact.

The direction is what matters. Every serious estimate points the same way: down. The
argument is about how far, not whether.

## The mistake was treating sovereignty as a switch

Here is the lesson, and it is small enough to carry.

Sovereignty is a setting, not a switch. You do not have to choose between full membership
and full exit. The whole range in between — aligned standards, mutual recognition,
shared rails with national control of the data and the keys — is where the value
lives. Brexit flipped a switch when it could have turned a dial.

Control and connection are not opposites. The expensive error is believing they are.

Engineers know this trade in their bones. You can fork the entire stack, rebuild every
dependency from scratch, and own all of it — and then carry the migration cost
forever, on every release, alone. Or you keep the shared substrate everyone else maintains
and own the one layer that actually decides your fate: governance. Where the data lives,
who holds the keys, whose law applies.

Brexit forked the stack. The cheaper, stronger move was to keep the substrate and own the
governance layer.

## Two roads to sovereignty

Hard exit

Control by separation

- Full legal autonomy on paper

- New friction at every border

- Lost market access, slower investment

- Cost: an estimated 4–6 percent of GDP

Deep interdependence

Control by design

- National control of data, keys, and law

- Shared standards and shared rails

- Market access kept, friction kept low

- Cost: governance work, not GDP

## Interdependence is the moat

Now point this at the bigger board. The US and the EU are not competitors deciding whether
to date. They are the two halves of the democratic industrial base — chips,
standards, capital, energy, defence, AI. The Coalition of the Willing stays top of the
game for one reason: it is wired together.

And "coalition" is the wrong word if you hear it as a treaty. The real alliance is not
political; it is economic. It is the set of jurisdictions and companies that picked
interoperability over purity. Switzerland never joined the EU and never cut itself off
from it — bilateral, sovereign, wired in. Singapore built a state on being the
connective tissue of Asian trade. The Nordic states sit inside European rails while
keeping their own currencies, their own defence calls, their own law.

Each of them is sovereign and connected. None confused independence with
strength. That confusion is the real losing trade — not just for the UK, but for
every autarky project that mistook the drawbridge for a wall.

A rival can copy a model. A rival can fund a fab. What a rival cannot quickly copy is a
dense web of allies who trade, set standards, and deploy on the same rails. That web is
the moat. Brexit's cautionary tale is what happens when you cut one strand of it on
purpose.

So the play for Washington and Brussels is not "decouple from each other to feel
sovereign." It is the opposite. Keep sovereignty where it belongs — data, law, the
keys to the kingdom — and deepen interdependence everywhere else. Sovereign at the
edge. Connected across the core.

## Even China is on the axis

Here is the test of the claim. If interdependence were only a Western comfort blanket, the
country that talks the most about self-reliance would have walked away from it. China has
not. It cannot — not cheaply.

Look at its alternatives. Full autarky means giving up the export markets, the foreign
capital, and the frontier technology it does not yet make at home. A parallel bloc —
its own standards, its own settlement rails, its own supply chains — is not an exit
from interdependence. It is a second web, thinner and younger than the one it would
replace. Neither option is independence. Both are just a different shape of dependence, at
a higher price.

So China sits on the same axis as everyone else. The dial, not the switch. Its version is
more state-directed and more coercive — and that is exactly the point. The
democratic coalition's edge is not that it is connected; China is connected too. The
edge is that its connections are rules-based and chosen. The moat is not the web alone. It
is a web allies trust.

## The dial, not the switch

This scales down as cleanly as it scales up. Every firm choosing where its data lives,
whose cloud it runs on, and which standards it speaks is making the same call Britain made
— just with fewer headlines. Fork everything and own a fragile island, or keep the
substrate and own the governance.

The ones who get it right do not pick between control and connection. They keep
sovereignty where it earns trust — the data, the keys, the law — and keep
interdependence where it earns the moat. Sovereign at the edge. Connected across the core.

Brexit paid 6 percent to learn that lesson out loud. The rest of us get to learn it for
free.

The short version

- Brexit asked the right question: who decides? Sovereignty is real value.

- It bought that sovereignty at the most expensive counter — an estimated 4 to 6
percent of GDP, permanent and compounding.

- The cost was asymmetric: pain immediate and concentrated, benefit diffuse and
theoretical. Sovereignty without interdependence has a negative return.

- The error was treating sovereignty as a switch. It is a setting — fork the stack
and pay forever, or keep the substrate and own the governance layer.

- The real coalition is economic, not political. Switzerland, Singapore, the Nordics
stayed sovereign and connected. Autarky confused independence with strength.

- Even China sits on the axis — its alternatives (autarky, a thinner parallel
bloc) cost more. The democratic edge is not being connected, but being connected on
rules allies trust.

- Sovereign at the edge. Connected across the core. Both, or neither works.
