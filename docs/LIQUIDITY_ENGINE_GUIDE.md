## 1. The Big Idea

Imagine you and your friends each put money into building the coolest lemonade stand in town. The dream was always: one day a big corporation buys the whole stand for millions. That's the **Exit** — the big payday everyone waited for.

But what if you didn't have to wait? What if you could sell pieces of the stand — one table, two chairs, the sign — while it's still running?

That's the **Liquidity Engine**.

---

## 2. The Old Way — Binary Exits

> "We build it. We wait. A big company buys it OR we sell shares to the whole city at once (that's the IPO). Everyone gets rich. The end."

It was binary — like a light switch. Either **ON** (big exit) or **OFF** (nothing).

The only two options were:

- **Acquisition** — A bigger company buys you outright
- **IPO** — You sell shares to the public on a stock exchange

Both required the company to reach a certain size, maturity, and market timing. Most never got there.

---

## 3. What Broke It

After 2021, interest rates went up. The "buy everything at crazy prices" party ended.

Now companies stay private way longer — sometimes **10–15 years**. The investors who put money in early are just… waiting. And waiting.

Meanwhile their own bosses (called **LPs** — think: the parents who gave them money to invest) are asking: *"Where's our money back?"*

That number — **DPI** (Distributions to Paid-In Capital) — basically means "how many actual dollars did you return to us per dollar we gave you."

Right now, DPI is terrible for most funds.

### The Math Problem

| Metric | Healthy | Current Reality |
|--------|---------|-----------------|
| DPI target by year 7 | 1.0x+ | 0.3x–0.5x |
| Average hold period | 5–7 years | 10–15 years |
| LP patience | Assumed infinite | Very much finite |

---

## 4. The New Way — The Liquidity Engine

Instead of one giant payday, people figured out you can **sell pieces of the lemonade stand while it's still running**.

| Old Way | New Way |
|---------|---------|
| Sell the whole stand at the end | Sell one table, then two chairs, then the sign |
| Wait 10 years for one big check | Get smaller checks every 2–3 years |
| Binary: win big or nothing | Continuous: always some money flowing |

That market for buying and selling pieces of private companies is called the **Secondary Market**.

---

## 5. How Big Is It Now?

In 2025, **$106 billion** worth of these "pieces" were bought and sold.

To put that in perspective:

- That's enough to buy every NBA team about **3 times over**
- It's almost as big as the whole IPO market ($120B) and M&A market ($140B)

It went from a weird side hustle to the **third biggest game in town**.

### Market Size Comparison (2025)

| Exit Channel | Volume | Trend |
|-------------|--------|-------|
| M&A | ~$140B | Flat |
| IPO | ~$120B | Recovering |
| **Secondary Market** | **$106B** | **Growing fast** |

Source: Industry estimates, 2025. source: 'Jefferies Global Secondary Market Review 2025, Lazard Secondary Market Report'

## 6. The Three Tools Investors Use

### 6.1 Tender Offers

The company lets early employees/investors sell some shares privately before any IPO.

**Analogy:** Selling your Pokémon cards to a collector before the tournament.

- Company-initiated, controlled process
- Usually at a set price (often a discount to latest round)
- Common at SpaceX, Stripe, OpenAI
- Employees get liquidity without waiting for IPO

### 6.2 Continuation Vehicles (CVs)

The fund manager says: "I'm not selling this company yet, but I'll let you cash out — I'll find new investors to buy your spot."

**Analogy:** Musical chairs with money.

- GP (fund manager) moves the best assets into a new fund
- Existing LPs can cash out or roll over
- New investors buy in at current valuation
- GP keeps managing the winners longer

### 6.3 GP-Led Restructurings

The fund basically resets itself, moving the best companies into a new fund so they can keep holding them longer.

- More complex than CVs
- Can involve multiple portfolio companies
- Often includes fresh capital for growth
- Resets the clock on fund lifecycle

### Comparison

| Tool | Who Initiates | Who Sells | Typical Discount |
|------|--------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Tender Offer | Company | Employees/early investors | 10–20% to last round |
| Continuation Vehicle | GP (fund manager) | Existing LPs | 5–15% |
| GP-Led Restructuring | GP (fund manager) | Existing LPs + restructuring | Varies |

## 7. The Power Law Problem

The really interesting twist: most of the gains are coming from just **a few giant winners** — OpenAI, SpaceX, Stripe.

These companies are worth hundreds of billions but haven't done an IPO yet.

Rich investors are already buying and selling pieces of OpenAI in the secondary market right now — **no IPO needed**. By the time OpenAI ever rings a bell on Wall Street, the early money will already have been paid back through secondaries.

### The Concentration Risk

| Company | Estimated Private Valuation (2025) | IPO Status |
|---------|-----------------------------------|------------|
| SpaceX | ~$350B | Private |
| OpenAI | ~$300B | Private |
| Stripe | ~$90B | Private |
| Databricks | ~$60B | Private |

These few names account for a disproportionate share of secondary market activity. If you're not in them, the liquidity engine runs slower.

---

## 8. Is This Permanent or Just a Band-Aid?

**It's structural.** Here's why:

1. **Companies don't need to go public anymore.** Private funding is abundant.
2. **The 10-year fund lifecycle is broken.** Companies need 15+ years now.
3. **Regulation made being public expensive.** Sarbanes-Oxley, quarterly reporting, activist investors.
4. **Secondary markets have professionalized.** More liquid, more trusted, better infrastructure every year.

The IPO isn't dead — it's just been **demoted from "the only exit" to "one option among several."**

### The Structural Shift

```
2010s: Build → Grow → IPO/Acquire (the only path)
2020s: Build → Grow → Secondary → More Growth → Maybe IPO → Maybe Not
```

## 9. The One-Sentence Summary

> **Before:** Investors were like farmers who could only sell their harvest once a year at one market.
>
> **Now:** They can sell any vegetable, any time, at dozens of markets — and some vegetables are worth more before they're fully grown.

The lemonade stand doesn't have to close for you to get your money back. That's the Liquidity Engine. 🚀

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **DPI** | Distributions to Paid-In Capital — how much cash returned per dollar invested |
| **GP** | General Partner — the fund manager who makes investment decisions |
| **LP** | Limited Partner — the investor who gives money to the GP to invest |
| **IPO** | Initial Public Offering — selling shares to the public on a stock exchange |
| **M&A** | Mergers and Acquisitions — one company buying another |
| **Secondary Market** | Market for buying/selling existing stakes in private companies |
| **Tender Offer** | Company-organized event where employees/investors can sell shares |
| **Continuation Vehicle** | New fund created to hold winning investments longer |
| **Power Law** | Pattern where a tiny number of investments generate most returns |
| **Sarbanes-Oxley** | US regulation (2002) that made being a public company more expensive |
