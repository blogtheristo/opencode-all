**Document Type:** Creed / Doctrine (draft)
**Scope:** A European counterpart to *The Technological Republic* (Karp & Zamiska, 2025). Written from European values — the Charter of Fundamental Rights, the rule-of-law tradition, subsidiarity, social market economy, total defence — rather than translated from American ones.
**Purpose:** To answer the question Karp chose not to answer: what does hard power built on soft belief look like when the soft belief is European?
**Status:** Draft. Meant to be attacked. Theses that cannot survive attack will be rewritten.

## Preamble

Europe is the working response to the twentieth century. Two world wars, the Shoah, the Gulag, Srebrenica, Bucha. Our values are not preferences and not aesthetics. They are lessons paid for in blood by people still alive. Any European creed has to start there, or it is a marketing document written by somebody who has not been to the archive.

What follows is a draft. Twenty-two theses, most uncomfortable, some costly. It is not the last word. It is a first word a European company is willing to put its name to.

---

## Part I — Foundation: What We Learned the Hard Way

### 1. Europe is not a mistake.

The European project is not a bureaucratic accident and not a second-best substitute for nation-states. It is the deliberate architecture built after 1945 to make a third European war impossible. Every critique of "Brussels" that forgets what Brussels was invented to prevent is ahistorical.

### 2. Rule of law precedes power.

Our first political question is not *who rules* but *under what law*. Power that is not under law is not European power, whatever flag it flies. This is the line between the Bundesrepublik and what preceded it, between the Fifth Republic and Vichy, between Poland after 1989 and Poland before.

### 3. Human dignity is the anchor.

Article 1 of the Charter: *"Human dignity is inviolable. It must be respected and protected."* Article 1 of the German Basic Law says the same, deliberately and first. Rights, markets, defence, technology — everything else is downstream of this sentence. A European stack that does not hold it is not European.

### 4. Peace was an engineering project.

Schuman and Monnet did not write a manifesto. They wired together coal and steel production so that war between France and Germany became mechanically impossible. European peace is a built artefact, not a mood. It needs to be maintained like any other piece of infrastructure.

### 5. The welfare state is mobilisation base, not decadence.

A state that asks its citizens for sacrifice must have kept faith with them first. Nordic conscription works because the state that calls up its young has also paid for their education, their healthcare, and their mother's pension. The welfare state is the precondition of credible European hard power, not its opposite.

## Part II — Sovereignty: What We Defend

### 6. Data protection is a fundamental right, not a compliance cost.

The GDPR is the Charter applied to silicon. A European cloud, a European model, a European agent that treats data protection as an obstacle is a European product in name only. This is why *noyb* and the EDPB matter more than most engineers realise.

### 7. Subsidiarity is the opposite of centralisation.

Decisions should be taken at the lowest level at which they can be taken well. This is a design constraint for every European platform, not a political slogan. Defence procurement, digital identity, and cultural policy are three places where European overreach has produced worse outcomes than restraint.

### 8. Sovereignty now includes the stack.

A nation that cannot run its critical systems without American cloud, American silicon, and American foundation models is not sovereign in 2026. Sovereignty in this century is layered: compute, storage, models, ontology, governance. Losing any layer loses the whole.

### 9. The CLOUD Act is a real threat, not a theoretical one.

United States law reaches United States operators regardless of where the data sits. A European defence, healthcare, or energy system running on a U.S.-owned operator has an extraterritorial backdoor written into statute. The question is not *whether* to mitigate. The question is *how*, and on what timeline, and at what cost.

### 10. "Made in Europe" is not chauvinism; it is option-value.

Europe does not need autarky. It needs the option to decouple if the alliance ever fails. That option is bought cheaply in peacetime and is impossible to buy in crisis. Every sovereign capability added in 2026 is insurance against a year we hope never arrives.

## Part III — Hard Power with European Characteristics

### 11. Pacifism is a luxury good.

For seventy years Europe could afford pacifism because the United States paid for the defence. That subsidy is ending whether we like it or not. European pacifism without American underwriting is not peace; it is outsourced deterrence with a falling credit rating.

### 12. Deterrence is moral.

Preventing war by making it unwinnable is not militarism. It is the lesson of 1939 and the lesson of February 2022. A Europe that cannot deter will be invaded, and the people who pay first are the ones with the weakest borders. Ukrainians, Estonians, and Finns understand this sentence in their bones.

### 13. Universal civic service belongs in the conversation.

Finland, Estonia, Sweden, Norway, Austria, Greece, Denmark, and Switzerland have kept or restored it. The European question is not *whether* to have civic service — it is *what kind, for whom, and into which institutions*. Defence, civil protection, care of the elderly, critical infrastructure. Everyone serves somewhere. Shared risk is the precondition of legitimate hard power.

### 14. Total defence is a European invention.

Finnish and Swiss doctrine — *the whole society as the defence* — is how small nations deter large ones. It is a template for the rest of Europe, not a Nordic curiosity. Private industry, civil society, universities, and reservists inside one mobilisation framework. This is already working. It should be copied.

### 15. AI in defence needs a European doctrine.

Karp's "who, not whether" is right about the question. The European answer to *who* is specific: under democratic oversight, under the Charter, with meaningful human control, with reasoning lineage logged (Article 12 of the EU AI Act), and without handing the kill chain to a foreign vendor. A European military AI that cannot be audited by a European parliament is a contradiction in terms.

## Part IV — The Uncomfortable Theses

### 16. Energy is the first domain of sovereignty.

A Europe dependent on Russian gas learned this at gunpoint in 2022. A Europe dependent on Gulf LNG, Chinese solar, or African uranium is making the same mistake with different suppliers. Energy autonomy — nuclear, wind, geothermal, storage, grid — is the ground floor beneath every other sovereignty. Get this wrong and the rest of the creed is theatre.

### 17. The border is not a policy; it is a precondition.

A polity that cannot decide who enters is not a polity. Saying this out loud is not far-right. Refusing to say it is how the centre of every European country has been hollowed out, seat by seat, since 2015. The humane answer and the secure answer are the same answer, and Europe has not yet built it. We owe this to refugees as much as to citizens.

### 18. Free speech is European before it is American.

Voltaire, Mill, the Enlightenment. Home soil. The recent European temptation to manage speech by vibe, by informal pressure on platforms, or by minister's letter, is a betrayal of our own tradition and not a correction of an American one. The Digital Services Act is defensible because it is law. Extra-legal pressure on platforms is not.

### 19. Religion is not the enemy of the European project.

The Charter preamble speaks of Europe's *"spiritual and moral heritage."* A secular Europe that cannot tolerate a practising Christian, Muslim, or Jew in public life is not the Europe the Charter describes. Secularism is the neutrality of the state, not the exclusion of the believer.

### 20. "Ever-closer union" is a direction, not a deadline.

Subsidiarity means some things should stay national. Defence industrial policy is a case where European overreach has produced duplication and waste; digital identity is another; cultural policy a third. A stronger Europe is sometimes a more modest Europe. The Union is credible when it does the things no member state can do alone, and silent when member states can manage.

## Part V — What We Are Building For

### 21. Inclusion into what?

Karp's thesis 22, asked honestly from this side of the Atlantic. The European answer is: inclusion into the polity of the Charter. That is a demanding answer. It requires the newcomer to accept human dignity, equality of the sexes, rule of law, and the rights of minorities, women, and apostates as non-negotiable — not as "Western preferences." Inclusion into *vibes* is cheap and fails within a generation. Inclusion into *constitutional values* is expensive and holds. Europe has been avoiding this sentence for forty years. It is the reason the far right has been eating the centre's lunch.

### 22. Europe is worth defending.

Not because it is perfect — it is not — but because the alternative is what came before 1945 and what exists today east of the Dnieper, south of the Bosphorus, and inside every authoritarian state our grandparents escaped. The people who wrote the Charter knew what they were escaping. A generation that has not read them is now being asked to defend what they built. That generation should read them, and then defend.

## Coda — The Standard for Ourselves

If this creed is worth anything, it has to apply to the company publishing it.

- **On thesis 3 (dignity):** every product we ship must pass a dignity test, not only a compliance test.
- **On thesis 8 (stack sovereignty):** every critical dependency must have a European substitute on the roadmap, or we must be honest that we are a US-stack reseller with European branding.
- **On thesis 15 (AI doctrine):** every agent we build must log its reasoning under Article 12, must preserve meaningful human control, and must be buildable by a European parliament's auditor.
- **On thesis 18 (free speech):** we will not take down lawful content on informal government request. Court order or statute, or nothing.
- **On thesis 21 (inclusion into what):** we will hire, contract, and partner with people who accept the Charter. We will not soften this for market access.

This is not aspirational. It is the minimum bar for being allowed to publish the creed at all. If we fail any of the five, the creed fails with us, and we owe the reader a public correction.

## Method Note — How This Was Written

Built backwards from the Charter of Fundamental Rights, the German Basic Law Article 1, the Finnish total-defence doctrine, and the Schuman Declaration. Tested against Karp & Zamiska's twenty-two theses to see which translate and which do not. Theses 1, 3, 6, 7, 9, 13, 14, 16, 17, 19 diverge substantively from the American original. Theses 4, 5, 10, 11, 12, 15, 20 are European restatements. Theses 2, 8, 18, 21, 22 are shared across both traditions but grounded in different texts.

Sources consulted: the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (2000/2009), Grundgesetz Art. 1 (1949), Schuman Declaration (9 May 1950), Finnish Security Strategy for Society (2017/2024), Swiss Federal Constitution Art. 58–61 (Total Defence), EU AI Act Regulation 2024/1689, GDPR Regulation 2016/679, Digital Services Act Regulation 2022/2065, CLOUD Act (USA, 2018).
