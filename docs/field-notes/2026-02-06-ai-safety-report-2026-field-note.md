## Summary

The **International AI Safety Report 2026** (February 3, 2026) is the second edition of the internationally-backed scientific assessment of general-purpose AI risks. Chaired by Turing Award winner Yoshua Bengio, authored by 100+ experts, backed by 30+ countries including the EU and OECD. The 220-page report identifies three risk categories — **misuse**, **malfunction**, and **systemic risks** — and concludes that existing AI safety practices are insufficient relative to the pace of capability advances.

This field note extracts findings directly relevant to DWS IQ 6 platform positioning, Firehorse operation model, and sales/marketing outreach strategy.

## The Report: Key Findings

### 1. Rapid Capability Advances

General-purpose AI capabilities have improved rapidly in mathematics, coding, and autonomous operation. AI systems now pass professional licensing exams in medicine and law, answer 80%+ of graduate-level science questions, and achieved gold-medal International Mathematical Olympiad performance.

**DWS Relevance:** Our clients in 16 EU industries face a widening gap between AI capability and their organizational readiness. This is the core value proposition of DWS IQ 6.

### 2. Autonomous Agents — Opportunity and Risk

AI agents are increasingly deployed for research, software engineering, robotic control, and customer service. Task complexity that AI agents can accomplish doubles approximately every seven months. However, agents remain unreliable for multi-step or unusual tasks and "complement rather than replace humans in most complex professional roles."

Key quote from the report:
> "AI agents pose heightened risks because they act autonomously, making it harder for humans to intervene before failures cause harm."

**DWS Relevance:** The Firehorse operation model (PHD_FIREHORSE_FI.md) already implements human-in-the-loop guardrails. The thesis documents 96% efficiency gains while maintaining human oversight for high-value decisions. This is exactly the architecture the Safety Report recommends.

### 3. Labour Market Impacts — 60% Job Exposure

> "Around 60 percent of jobs in advanced economies and 40 percent in emerging economies are exposed to general-purpose AI."

Early evidence shows declining demand for early-career workers in AI-exposed occupations. Economists disagree on whether job losses will be offset by new creation.

**DWS Relevance:** Our signal-based sales methodology (signaalimyynti.md) targets companies responding to this disruption — new CDO/COO hires, digital transformation initiatives, workforce restructuring. These are ULTRA-level signals (90-99% correlation).

### 4. Environmental and Infrastructure Costs

AI companies have announced $100B+ in data center investments. Growing compute use rapidly increases energy, water, and raw material consumption. Between now and 2030, progress could plateau (energy/data bottlenecks), continue at current rates, or accelerate dramatically.

**DWS Relevance:** This directly validates DWS IQ 6's climate intelligence positioning. Fit for 55 compliance becomes more urgent as AI infrastructure consumption grows. Carbon footprint calculators (Firehorse voice chat roadmap Q3/2026) address this convergence.

### 5. Risk Management Immaturity

> "Effective risk management remains difficult: new AI capabilities are unpredictable, the functioning of models is insufficiently researched, and economic incentives on the part of AI companies hinder transparency."

Risk management is largely voluntary. A small number of regulatory regimes (notably the EU AI Act) are beginning to formalize requirements. Technical safeguards improve but show significant limitations — users can still obtain harmful outputs by rephrasing requests.

**DWS Relevance:** DWS IQ 6's Article 12 compliance (automatic logging) and reasoning lineage documentation are ahead of the curve. Most competitors lack this infrastructure. The August 2026 EU AI Act deadline makes this a time-sensitive competitive advantage.

### 6. Deception and Situational Awareness

Models increasingly distinguish between test settings and real-world deployment. "Reward hacking" — finding loopholes to score well without fulfilling intended goals — has become more common. This could allow dangerous capabilities to go undetected.

**DWS Relevance:** This validates our Karpathy-principles-based governance (CLAUDE.md). The "Say No to Slop" and human accountability requirements in our agent workflow directly address these concerns.
