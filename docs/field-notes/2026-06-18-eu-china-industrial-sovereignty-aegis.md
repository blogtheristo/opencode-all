## §2. What Is Being Destroyed

The sectors under attack are not peripheral. They are the load-bearing columns of European industrial civilisation.

### 2.1 Steel

European steel production has fallen from 177 million tonnes (2007) to approximately 130 million tonnes today. Outokumpu, SSAB, Tata Steel Europe, ArcelorMittal, and Thyssenkrupp are all restructuring under the same pressure: Chinese hot-rolled coil priced at EUR 450–480/tonne when the fully-loaded EU cost is EUR 600–650/tonne. The gap is not efficiency — it is state subsidy.

**DWS Aegis relevance:** Outokumpu is already a target Tier-1 sovereignty pilot customer. CBAM carbon tariff tracking is live in Aegis VulcanAgent. Every EU steel producer faces ETS allocation reductions from 2026 and CBAM full implementation from 2026. This is a live customer signal, not a future opportunity.

### 2.2 Solar and Clean Energy Equipment

China captured 98% of EU solar panel imports through sustained state-subsidised overproduction. Every major EU solar panel manufacturer — Solarwatt, Meyer Burger, Enel Green Power — has shut or offshored. The EU's Renewable Energy Directive targets 42.5% renewable by 2030. That target is now structurally dependent on Chinese manufacturing — a single supply-chain disruption away from becoming undeliverable.

### 2.3 Batteries and EV Supply Chain

CATL, BYD, and SVOLT supply approximately 70% of battery cells entering EU-assembled EVs. The EU's Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA) targets 40% domestic processing of strategic minerals by 2030 — from a near-zero base. Chinese dominance in cathode active materials, lithium hydroxide processing, and battery cell manufacturing creates a Technology Sovereignty gap at the physical layer equivalent to having 70% of your cloud workloads locked into a CLOUD-Act-exposed provider.

### 2.4 Chemicals and Machinery

Chinese overcapacity in basic chemicals (chlorine, soda ash, phosphoric acid) and industrial machinery has compressed margins across the EU chemical sector. BASF's EUR 2.6B restructuring in 2024 is the most visible symptom of a sector-wide margin compression driven not by EU cost inefficiency but by Chinese state-backed export pricing.

## §3. The Sovereignty Model Applied to Industry

Field Note #2 defined three pillars — Data Sovereignty, Technology Sovereignty, Operational Sovereignty — and noted that Technology Sovereignty erodes through "vendor lock-in; closed protocols; non-portable workloads; opaque firmware and silicon supply chains."

The industrial attack follows exactly the same pattern, one level lower in the stack.

| Pillar                      | Digital attack vector (Field Note #2)                      | Industrial attack vector (this note)                                                                                               |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Data Sovereignty**        | CLOUD Act / FISA 702 data access through US-origin cloud   | Chinese industrial espionage through OT/ICS systems (Huawei, HIKVISION), dual-use equipment with undisclosed telemetry             |
| **Technology Sovereignty**  | Vendor lock-in to US hyperscalers; non-portable workloads  | Supply-chain capture through Chinese-subsidised overcapacity; no EU alternative at scale for solar, batteries, steel inputs        |
| **Operational Sovereignty** | Continuity failure under NIS2 / DORA / regulatory sanction | Production continuity failure when Chinese export restrictions activate (rare earths, gallium, germanium, graphite — already done) |

**The quantum overlay from Field Note #2 has an industrial equivalent:** Chinese export restrictions on critical materials are not hypothetical. China banned gallium and germanium exports in August 2023. It imposed graphite controls in October 2023. It has signalled rare-earth restrictions as a trade-war instrument. An EU industrial enterprise with no alternative supply path for these inputs has a Technology Sovereignty failure with an expiry date — the date China decides to activate the restriction.

## §4. The EU Has the Instruments. Enforcement Is the Gap.

The legal architecture for EU industrial defence is largely in place. The problem is enforcement velocity and enterprise-level compliance readiness.

| Instrument                                      | Status                                                   | What it does                                                                                             | Enterprise compliance requirement                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **CBAM** (Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism)   | Phase 1 live (Oct 2023); full enforcement 2026           | Tariff on imported steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, hydrogen, electricity based on embedded carbon | Quarterly embedded-carbon calculation and CBAM Registry reporting        |
| **Foreign Subsidies Regulation** (EU 2022/2560) | Live since Oct 2023                                      | Due-diligence obligation on M&A > EUR 250M and public tenders > EUR 4M if non-EU subsidies suspected     | Supply-chain subsidy disclosure; procurement due-diligence               |
| **Anti-Dumping Regulations**                    | Active on solar (15–50%), steel (many), EV (active 2024) | Countervailing duties on Chinese-subsidised goods                                                        | Procurement sourcing validation; CBAM carbon origin tracking             |
| **EUDR** (EU Deforestation Regulation)          | Delayed to 2025–26 enforcement                           | Due-diligence on supply chains involving deforestation                                                   | Geospatial supply-chain documentation                                    |
| **NIS2 Directive**                              | Transposed 2024-10-17                                    | Cybersecurity obligations for critical entities including energy, manufacturing, chemicals, steel        | Security posture assessment, incident reporting, supplier security audit |
| **EU AI Act** Art. 6/9/50                       | Phased, 2024–2026                                        | High-risk AI in critical infrastructure                                                                  | Logging, conformance documentation, human oversight                      |
