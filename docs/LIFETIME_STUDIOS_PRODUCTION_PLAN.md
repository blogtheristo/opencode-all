## 1. Purpose and Status

This document is the **BLOG_STANDARD annex** governing the systematic production of 800 flagship stories (kantajuttu) as individual **Business Case cards**. It is an **internal production and quality standard**: it is version-controlled, defines the source of truth for Rule 13 (named reviewer), the content matrix, the card template, roles, quality gates, and KPI tracking — and it must **not** be published as an external promise until explicitly released.

The production engine is not a content farm. Each Business Case feeds the DWS ontology and graph, which feeds an enterprise's own World Model, which feeds an industry demo, which feeds a Captain conversation, which becomes a paid pilot. The customer buys access to an evolving industrial compliance environment, not a single report.

## 2. The Production Chain

The end-to-end chain is fixed:

**Matrix row → cluster company → sources and claims → card → HIC review → journalistic gate → five language versions → blog, LinkedIn and Facebook → ontology feedback → demo → Captain.**

Every step has an owner (§9), and no step may be skipped or reordered. The **source and claim map** (step 3, card field 8) is what keeps the journalistic gate and the demo bound together: the demo demonstrates exactly what the sourced claims support.

## 3. The 800-Story Structure (200-Day Program)

| Definition | Count | Explanation |
| ---------- | ----- | ----------- |
| Core topics (matrix rows) | 125 | 25 industries × 5 pain points |
| Flagship stories (cards) | 800 | Individual Business Case cards, not 800×5 versions |
| Language versions | ≈4,000 | 800 × 5 (FI/SV/DE/FR/EN) |
| Enterprise account clusters | ≈4,000 | Fit for 55 target companies, buyer/data/regulation/product viewpoints |

**Composition:** 800 = 125 core cards (thorough public research per matrix row) + 675 variations (different buyer, situation, or product that reuse the core research).

This is **not a 3.2-year calendar project**. The **125 core cards are produced first** as the research and structure phase that establishes the business framework for the whole product family. The **675 variations are then produced at sales-pipeline priority**, not by the calendar. **Core-card priority decides.**

The **200-day target** is reached with a **parallel production line**: research, writing, HIC review, localization, and publication run simultaneously (800 stories / 200 days ≈ **4 flagship stories per day** on average).

## 6. Business Case Card — Eight Mandatory Fields

Every matrix row fixes exactly these fields **before writing begins**:

1. One industry and one enterprise buyer
2. One precise compliance situation
3. One visible financial or operational consequence
4. One DWS IQ product or technology solving the bounded problem
5. One demo workflow
6. One acceptance criterion or measurable outcome
7. One next step: demo, discovery, or paid pilot
8. **Source and claim map** — every fact and claim mapped to its primary public source (or the company's own data / ontology / World Model evidence), with sourced facts separated from analysis and hypotheses

The **source and claim map** is mandatory because it keeps the journalistic gate and the demo bound together: without it, the demo can claim what the sources do not support. Field 8 is produced in step 3 of the production chain, before the card is written.

**Example (Outokumpu template):** "A stainless-steel producer, group compliance lead, installation-level emissions evidence missing from the CBAM and procurement chain, Aegis/AASS produces a traceable evidence packet, demo shows 304L/Tornio data handling and ERP transfer, next step is a 30-day paid pilot."

## 8. Production Pipeline (per story)

1. **Target selection** — matrix row × cluster company (sales-pipeline priority)
2. **Public research** — company reports, EU legislation, authority data; primary sources only
3. **Source and claim map** (card field 8) — every fact and claim mapped to a primary source, sourced facts separated from analysis/hypotheses
4. **Draft** (agent) → **HIC review** (Rule 13 — no named reviewer, no publication in any version)
5. **Journalistic gate** (Outokumpu model): facts/claims/analysis/hypotheses separated; no law-breaking claims without public evidence; comment opportunity before publication; no implied endorsement
6. **Localization** — five languages → blog post + LinkedIn post + Facebook long-tail
7. **Publication** — onelifetime.world/posts/ + landing-pages mirror + `scripts/update-blog-index.mjs` (idempotent)
8. **Distribution** — class-1 pool (fi→Facebook, en→LinkedIn) scheduled
9. **Ontology feedback** — card structure feeds the DWS ontology/graph

An agent may produce a draft; every one of the ≈4,000 versions requires a named human reviewer (§10).

## 10. Named Reviewer Capacity Budget

The constraint of the 4,000-language-version model is **one named reviewer per version**. An agent can produce the draft, but **review capacity and approval must be budgeted visibly**:

- **Review load:** 20 language versions/day + 4 LinkedIn + 4 Facebook versions/day = ≈28 approvals/day minimum, on top of the 4 flagship-story HIC reviews.
- **Budgeting:** named reviewers per language must be assigned with a defined daily review quota and an approval SLA; capacity is planned before production, not discovered during it.
- **Rule 13:** no version is published in any language without a named reviewer. Agent drafts are never self-approved.

Reviewer capacity is the scarcest resource in the system and is planned first.

## 13. Production Strategy — Repetition vs. Proof

It is often claimed that a person needs about 40 repetitions before believing a claim. The idea is useful from a marketing perspective, but **40 is not a universal magic number.** In research the phenomenon is known as the **illusory truth effect**: repeating a claim increases its familiarity and can increase its perceived truthfulness even when the claim is false. The original study establishing the effect was published in 1977, and the research evidence shows that **even a single repetition already significantly increases the perceived believability of a claim**.¹ The effect's intensity does not grow linearly up to 40 repetitions — the marginal benefit of repetition flattens much sooner (commonly already after roughly 3–5 repetitions), even though repetition itself continues to strengthen the memory trace.²

The operating model for Lifetime Studios follows from this: **the same verified core claim should appear in about 40 touchpoints, but in different forms and different contexts.** This is not 40 identical posts; it is the multifaceted reinforcement of one idea. A blog, an industry case, a white paper, a seminar, a LinkedIn post, a Facebook piece, a book chapter, and a demo can all convey the same core message to different buyer personas in different situations. The 40-touchpoint figure is a **distribution target across varied formats**, not a threshold after which belief begins.

The repeated claim must always be **bounded, justified, and sourced**. Repetition strengthens the memory trace, but it does not make a weak claim true — and the research shows prior knowledge does not by itself protect a reader from the effect.³ Lifetime Studios' advantage here is exceptionally strong: the same claim can be anchored to public sources, to the company's own data, to the ontology, and to the World Model's company-specific evidence. Fact-checking and providing evidence bound and reduce the misleading force of the effect,⁴ and truthful claims benefit from repetition more, and on more durable grounds, when they are combined with accuracy and verifiability.⁵ Thus the repetition rests not on visibility but on **provability** — the core argument is the difference between repetition and proof.

> **Canonical HIC wording (Suomi):** "Toistettavan väitteen on kuitenkin aina oltava rajattu, perusteltu ja lähteistetty. Toisto vahvistaa muistijälkeä, mutta ei tee heikosta väitteestä totta. Tässä Lifetime Studiosin etu on poikkeuksellisen vahva: sama väite voidaan ankkuroida julkisiin lähteisiin, yrityksen omaan dataan, ontologiaan sekä World Modelin tuottamaan yrityskohtaiseen evidenssiin. Näin toisto ei perustu näkyvyyteen vaan todistettavuuteen."

**Lifetime Studios principle:** *repetition builds recall, evidence earns trust.* — *Toisto rakentaa muistijäljen, evidenssi ansaitsee luottamuksen.* Repetition may start to work after a single exposure, so every claim must be sourced, bounded, and easily verifiable from the very first touchpoint — not after the 40th.

**Commercial credibility principle:** *truth first, both sides visible, useful analysis for the decision-maker, commercial step only when it genuinely follows from the problem.* — *Totuus ensin, molemmat puolet näkyviin, päätöksentekijälle hyödyllinen analyysi, kaupallinen askel vain kun se seuraa aidosti ongelmasta.* The journalistic work reports what the sources show, where uncertainty lies, and how different companies solve the same problem; the commercial connection is drawn only afterwards. If the analysis helps a CxO understand risk, data, and decision options better, an industry-specific demo is the natural next step. Repetition strengthens recall; sources, the handling of opposing views, and explicit uncertainty build trust. Content can therefore be journalistically honest and commercially effective at the same time.

### 13.0 Practical Editorial Rule

**DWS Content Production Standard.** The protected-argumentation model below is the **DWS content production standard**: the same structure runs across all 125 core cards and their variations, keeping the journalistic work credible while making the commercial value visible. In one line: *surface the limitation, open the effect, show the management measure, bound the uncertainty, offer the demo only as a natural continuation.*

Every card and post applies four editorial obligations:

1. **Address limits, uncertainties, and alternative interpretations** alongside the case — no one-sided selling.
2. **Separate the source, the company's own claim, the journalistic analysis, and the Lifetime-product hypothesis** (the four are never conflated).
3. **Give the reader enough information to draw their own conclusion** — the article informs; it does not decide for the reader.
4. **Keep the demo a possible next step, not a hidden conclusion of the article** — the commercial step must follow genuinely from the problem, not be grafted on.

**Protected-argumentation model (limitation handling):** *Limitation → effect on the decision → management measure → residual uncertainty → possible demo.* Every limitation is walked through the model explicitly. Synthesized as a practical editorial structure, the chain is:

1. **Identify and name the limitation** — *voluntary openness creates the basis for trust.* Place the limitation (e.g., *"product-level emissions data does not cover the entire Scope 3 supply chain"*) early in the analysis. This activates the reader's critical assessment without defences.
2. **Define the effect on the decision** — *a realistic assessment of the situation.* Describe without understatement or exaggeration what the limitation means in practice (e.g., *"the prepared evidence package therefore remains partial in regulatory enforcement"*).
3. **Present a clear management measure** — *turning the limitation into a bounded functional solution.* Demonstrate how the problem is handled methodologically (e.g., *"the use case is bounded precisely, missing data points are marked explicitly, and the audit is anchored to a source-specific evidence chain"*).
4. **Acknowledge the residual uncertainty** — *protects the article's expert value.* Make clear the limit up to which the analysis or solution carries (e.g., *"the result is not to be treated as a complete compliance solution, but as stepwise decision support"*).
5. **Offer the demo as a natural continuation** — *a consequence, not a hidden lead-in.* Present the demo only as a means to examine the management measure in practice (e.g., *"if you want to see how gaps, sources, and approvals are modelled in a workflow, an industry-specific demo illustrates this"*).

**Attribution framing.** Handling limitations voluntarily and proactively makes the reader perceive an honest actor (**internal attribution**) rather than a pure sales motive (**external attribution**), maximizing source credibility without diminishing the product's value. This is a textbook application of attribution theory combined with protected argumentation in a B2B context.

> **Editorial principle:** *do not hide the limitation, do not exaggerate it, show how it is managed.* — *Älä piilota rajoitetta, älä paisuttele sitä, näytä miten sitä hallitaan.*

> **Standard in brief (Suomi):** *Rajoite näkyviin, vaikutus auki, hallintakeino näytettäväksi, epävarmuus rajattava ja demo tarjottava vain luonnollisena jatkona.* Sama rakenne ajetaan kaikille 125 ydinkortille ja niiden variaatioille. Se pitää journalistisen työn uskottavana samalla kun se tekee kaupallisen arvon näkyväksi. Demo ei ole myyntipuheen päätepiste vaan tapa testata esitettyä hallintakeinoa käytännössä.

> **Two-sided caveat (source classification and boundary conditions).** The research direction is supported, but the sources must be classified correctly. Crowley & Hoyer (1994) present the *two-sided persuasion* framework; Holliman & Rowley (2014) study B2B content-marketing *practices*; Brehm & Brehm (1981) present the *theory of psychological reactance*; Pulizzi (2013) is a practical marketing book, **not** peer-reviewed research. The mechanism has precise boundary conditions, fully consistent with the scientific consensus: acknowledging a weakness raises **source credibility**, but if the stated limitation concerns a function critical to the product with no management measure, it can weaken the overall **brand attitude**. Two-sided communication works best when the raised limitation concerns a **secondary attribute or a risk for which a clear management measure or compensating benefit is offered** — it then raises the communicator's credibility without weakening the product evaluation.⁶ Eisend's broad meta-analysis confirms that presenting the other side (the limitations) significantly raises source credibility and reduces counterargument formation, but its effect on the final choice depends on **how the limitation is framed** — and is conditional on factors including the amount of negative information, its placement, the quality of the attribute, the ratio of positive to negative claims, and whether the information is presented voluntarily.¹¹ Therefore: the limitation must be **essential, honestly described, and paired with how the risk is managed** — only then does honesty build trust without undermining the offer.

The combination of journalistic practice and commercial effectiveness rests on the psychology of persuasion. Two-sided argumentation — stating limits and uncertainties alongside the case — raises perceived honesty and trustworthiness far more than one-sided sales talk.⁶ For B2B and CxO buyers, expert content with an editorial voice (third-party / editorial credibility) is perceived as considerably more trustworthy than conventional advertising and reduces the buyer's critical resistance.⁷ Value-creating, honest, decision-supporting content is essential for building purchase trust before the sales event itself.⁸ Communication built on openness and facts reduces the psychological reactance that direct sales pressure typically increases.⁹ The commercial result is not produced by pressure, but by offering the audience such high-quality information that a product demo or collaboration is the logical and desired next step.¹⁰
