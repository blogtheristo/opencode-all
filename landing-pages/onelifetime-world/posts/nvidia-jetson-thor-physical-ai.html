Physical AI · Partner Note

The supercomputer moves onto the robot.

NVIDIA Jetson Thor puts a Blackwell supercomputer in a 130 W box the robot carries. The silicon is theirs. The governance sits beside it.

26 Jun 2026 · by Risto Anton Paarni

The note, in one line

Jetson Thor is the box the robot wears — a Blackwell-class brain for physical AI, generally available, in a 130 W envelope. We cheer it. And for the regulated physical-AI work we serve, a faster brain on the robot does not change who owns it, where its logs live, or who holds the leash. Thor makes the body capable. Governance makes it deployable.

Good news from a good partner.

We have written before that the sovereign edge is still a box you own. Jetson Thor is that box, made concrete. A 2560-core Blackwell GPU, a 14-core Arm Neoverse CPU, 128 GB of memory, up to 2070 FP4 teraflops — on the robot, not in a rack across an ocean.

And because cheering without thinking is not a business, here is the honest part.

What NVIDIA actually shipped

The Jetson AGX Thor Developer Kit and the Jetson T5000 production module are now generally available. NVIDIA calls Thor the platform for physical AI — generalist robots that reason, plan, and act, rather than specialist machines reprogrammed for each job.

The numbers are real. Up to 7.5× the AI compute and 3.5× the energy efficiency of Jetson Orin. A Blackwell Transformer Engine that switches between FP4 and FP8 on the fly. Multi-Instance GPU, so a single chip partitions into isolated slices — critical workloads reserved, less urgent ones running beside them. JetPack 7 on Linux kernel 6.8 and Ubuntu 24.04, with a unified CUDA 13.0 across Arm targets. The developer kit lands at $3,499, and the platform runs Isaac GR00T, Holoscan, Metropolis, and the new Cosmos Reason VLM. We are reading it at the level NVIDIA wrote it — a platform direction for the robot’s body, not a spec sheet to recite.

Why Thor matters to us

We build on NVIDIA on purpose. The silicon, the drivers, the foundation models — that is the credibility layer. A capable, power-frugal brain on the robot is the thing that makes a governed control loop possible at the edge in the first place.

NVIDIA frames a humanoid as four layers, each at its own clock: hardware abstraction, a real-time control framework at 100 Hz–1 kHz, perception and planning at 30 Hz, and high-level reasoning at 1–5 Hz. That map is honest, and it is the same map our world-model loop reads onto. Thor supplies the compute for all four. We supply what the top layer needs to be trusted in a regulated room: a leash, a log, and a human who can say no.

Credit where it is due: Thor does not start the trust story from zero. JetPack 7 ships a hardware root of trust on the module itself — Secure Boot, a Trusted Execution Environment, disk and memory encryption, a firmware TPM, measured boot. That is real, and it is exactly the floor a governed edge needs to stand on. It secures the box. It does not, on its own, govern the decisions the box makes. Those are two different jobs.

What a faster brain on the robot does not change

High-level reasoning is the layer where a model decides what to do next. In a regulated setting, that layer is exactly the one the law watches hardest.

GDPR Article 5 and Schrems II, NIS2 24-hour incident reporting, EU AI Act Article 12 immutable logs, machinery-safety duties on an actuated robot — none of these are solved by more teraflops. They are solved by ownership, jurisdiction, an auditable record of every decision, and a governed handoff between the model and the person accountable for it.

Where Thor fits in our stack

One robot, two halves. NVIDIA owns the silicon and the runtime. We sit on top, where the decisions get governed.

The body · NVIDIA Jetson Thor

Blackwell GPU, real-time kernel, MIG isolation, Holoscan sensor fusion. The compute that runs perception, control, and reasoning on the robot itself. NVIDIA’s ground, and a good one to stand on.

The leash · the governed edge

A Situation Room the customer owns, with KYA trust scoring on every agent, immutable AI Act logs, and a human in the loop. Jurisdiction stays put. The record stays local.

We do not compete with the body. We would not try. We sit beside it — the layer that decides whether an action is allowed, records that it happened, and hands control back to a person when the room demands it. That is the half a faster chip cannot serve, and the half European operators of actuated machines keep asking about.

Why we will not over-claim

NVIDIA matched its own careful language. “The ultimate platform for physical AI” is a claim about the compute that powers a robot — not a claim that the robot is safe, compliant, or fit to act unsupervised. Those are earned downstream, in the deployment. We will keep the same restraint.

A developer kit is not a deployed system. A benchmark is not an audit. We will not say a governed control loop is faster than Thor — it runs on Thor. We say the decisions stay owned, the logs stay local, and a person stays accountable. That is enough.

The short version

  • Jetson Thor puts a Blackwell supercomputer in a 130 W box on the robot — generally available, $3,499 dev kit. Real, and good for everyone building physical AI.
  • NVIDIA’s four-layer map (hardware, control, perception, reasoning) reads cleanly onto our world-model loop. Thor runs all four.
  • The reasoning layer is where the law watches hardest. More teraflops do not solve GDPR, NIS2, or AI Act Article 12. Ownership, jurisdiction, and an auditable record do.
  • We do not compete with the body. We are the leash, the log, and the human in the loop.
  • Thor makes the body capable. EU-sovereign governance makes it deployable.
