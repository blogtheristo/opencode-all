KYA-S1 — one signed page for the agent in your vendor file

6 August 2026 · Risto Anton Päärni · Espoo, Finland

Update (10 August 2026): KYA-S1 is a specification, not a standard. The current version is v1.5, which added the TRS-4, TRS-5, PRV-4, TC4-5 and FLT-4 controls and renamed the assurance levels to KYA-AL1 / KYA-AL2 / KYA-AL3. Version numbers named in this post refer to what was current when it was published.

The news, in one line

Today we publish KYA-S1 v1.2 — a vendor specification for autonomous-agent identity and behavior, written so a single signed page can sit in your procurement folder next to SOC 2 and ISO 27001. Draft, open for public comment until 4 November 2026.

The opener

Procurement keeps a folder. Every enterprise vendor lives in it.

SOC 2 Type 2. ISO 27001. FedRAMP, where it applies. CSRD evidence. A signed page per line, and a date for the next renewal. That folder is how an enterprise decides who is allowed inside the network.

Autonomous agents need a line in that folder. There was not yet a standard that fit the slot. So we wrote one.

What lands in the folder

One page. Signed by the vendor and by a qualified independent assessor. Renewed on a fixed cadence. It carries the vendor and assessor names, the assessment date, the seven controls attested, the propagation SLAs, and a renewal date.

Two signatures. Seven controls, each ticked by someone who is not us. A renewal date the procurement team can put in a calendar.

That is what KYA-S1 is for. The rest of the specification exists to make that page mean something.

The seven things the assessor checks

Each control is a question the buyer asks before the agent gets to act on their data.

IDV — Identity Verification. "Is every action traceable to a verified human?" Biometric verification at session start. Cryptographic binding between the human, the agent, and every downstream tool call. Re-attestation on privilege change. Revocation propagates across the fleet inside the tier's SLA.
BHM — Behavioral Modeling. "Has the agent been red-teamed for what attackers actually try?" A behavioral baseline per agent class. Continuous adversarial simulation against prompt injection, MCP poisoning, agent-chain hijack, and jailbreak via chained tool calls. Guardrails block deviations at runtime and emit signed telemetry on every block.
REG — Agent Registry. "Can you tell me, right now, every agent touching our data?" A live registry of every agent instance, the human it is bound to, the capabilities it declared before activation, and the model version it runs. Queryable across the whole fleet. Works disconnected and reconciles on sync.
TC4 — Trust Collapse and Chain Termination. "When something goes wrong, how fast can you stop it?" Detect the collapse, terminate the offending agent and every agent it spawned, propagate fleet-wide inside the SLA, and preserve the decision trace for the post-mortem.
FLT — Fault Attribution. "If we end up in court, who is responsible for what?" A defined taxonomy — model, logic, integration, operator, policy — applied to every incident within 72 hours of close. Liability stops being an argument and becomes a lookup.
TRS — Trust Score. "Is authorization continuous, or did someone tick a box last quarter?" A live quantitative score per session. Consequential actions are blocked when the score falls below documented thresholds. Every score change is logged with the inputs that moved it.
PRV — Provenance. "Can you replay the decision, two years from now, for an auditor?" Every stored fact carries its source, timestamp, confidence and policy version. Every consequential decision is replayable from inputs, state, model version and policy version.

What the EU AI Act changed on 2 August 2026

Article 50 started to apply that day. That is worth being precise about, because three different duties are involved and they are not interchangeable.

Article 12 is record-keeping — logging sufficient for traceability across the system's lifetime. Article 14 is human oversight — a natural person who can understand, oversee and override. Article 50 is marking and labelling — 50(2) binds providers to machine-readable marking, and 50(4) binds deployers to label deep fakes and AI text published to inform the public on matters of public interest where nobody assumed editorial responsibility.

KYA-S1 sits near all three. PRV is nearest to Article 12. IDV, TC4 and TRS are nearest to Article 14. PRV-3 watermarking is nearest to Article 50.

Near is not the same as equal, and we say so in the specification. A KYA-S1 attestation produces evidence relevant to those articles. It does not discharge them. Any vendor — us included — who says "we are KYA-S1 aligned, therefore we are EU AI Act compliant" is selling something that is not true. Article 50 has three exceptions the specification does not encode, and whether a system is high-risk at all is a separate assessment that enforces from December 2027.

Why no self-attestation, including our own

Anyone can write a standard. The standard is only worth the assessor who signs against it.

So KYA-S1 disallows self-attestation in every control domain. The evidence has to be a third-party audit, signed platform telemetry, architecture documentation reviewed by an independent assessor, or a contractual SLA with a defined measurement method. Pick at least one, per domain, per year.

And the rule binds the house that wrote it. Lifetime Oy will not self-issue a KYA-S1 letter for the DWS IQ Platform. Until an independent assessor signs one, we claim no tier. What is in the specification under our name is an architecture description, not an attestation.

Bronze, Silver, Gold

The tiers differ mostly in how many seconds it takes a vendor to do the hard things: revoke a verified human, terminate a compromised chain, propagate that across the fleet. Bronze is fifteen minutes. Silver is five. Gold is sixty seconds, with an independent assessor across all seven domains.

A vendor claiming KYA-S1 names the tier, the assessor and the assessment date in the same line. Anything less is marketing.

The short version

Autonomous agents take consequential action, so procurement needs written accountability: who is behind the agent, what it can do, and how it is stopped.

KYA-S1 is the one signed page that answers those three questions — seven controls, two signatures, a renewal date.

No self-attestation. The page is only worth the name on the second signature line — and we hold ourselves to that before we ask anyone else to.

Draft open for public comment until 4 November 2026.

For engineers — how this sits next to other standards

SOC 2 and ISO 27001 attest the organisation. ISO/IEC 42001 attests the AI management system. FedRAMP attests the platform. Software supply-chain regimes — SBOM standards, vulnerability management, including Palantir's recently published MA-S2 — attest the code. KYA-S1 attests the agent running on top of all of it.

The four core domains were structured to read easily for engineers already working with supply-chain attestation regimes. That is the whole of the relationship: structural inspiration and public credit. No joint programme, no co-attestation, nothing agreed with Palantir.

The three extension domains — fault attribution, continuous trust score, decision provenance — have no analogue in any of those layers, because the problems they answer only exist once an autonomous agent is the actor.
