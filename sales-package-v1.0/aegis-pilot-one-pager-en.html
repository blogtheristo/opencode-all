Who held decision authority over your AI agents, and what followed?

When an AI agent works in a control room, the question afterwards is not whether a regulation was met. It is who held decision authority and what followed from it. The Aegis Pilot answers that with a signed record, on your hardware, with the network cut.

The stop is verified from the log, not from the agent's own report.

What the pilot proves in three months

At the end, your team cuts the uplink by a method you control. The result is binary. All three must hold:

  1. The piloted workflow completes without a network connection.
  2. The decision log keeps being written locally, without gaps.
  3. An operator stops a running agent, and after the halt moment no event from that agent is logged and no write reaches a managed resource.

The test agent is built to refuse to stop. A pass means it was stopped, not that it agreed to stop. A fail is a useful finding, learned for €19,990 rather than after a hardware programme.

What you keep

  • A signed acceptance record your auditor can verify on their own machine, without us.
  • Per agent: the authority it acted under, its decisions, the outcome, and who stopped it.
  • Evidence of the kind EU AI Act Articles 12 and 26 ask for. Whether it is sufficient is your auditor's call.
  • Production sizing from measured load, and a recommendation.

Scope, stated plainly

  • One site, one control-room workflow.
  • Agents produce decisions, recommendations and records. They do not actuate physical equipment; your plant's own emergency stop remains a separate layer.
  • Runs on your infrastructure. Keys and log stay with you. Each step runs in an NVIDIA OpenShell sandbox (open source; NVIDIA is not a party to this offer).
