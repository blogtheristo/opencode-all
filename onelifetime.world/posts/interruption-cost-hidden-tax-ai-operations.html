The Interruption Cost Crisis
— The Hidden Tax on AI Operations

Your per-token bill shows what you spent. It does not show what you lost. Every context switch — a sprint change, a key-person departure, a priority reshuffle — triggers five hidden cost layers that can consume over 30% of your annual team budget. We built a calculator to make them visible.

The Five Hidden Cost Drivers

When an AI-augmented team is interrupted, the cost is not the idle hours. It is the compound effect of five distinct cost layers, each invisible in standard billing:

1. Human Ramp-Up Cost

Each interruption forces the team to re-orient. Context is lost. People spend hours rebuilding mental models of where the project was. For a 50-person enterprise team with a 60-day recovery window, ramp-up hours alone represent a six-figure annual cost.

2. Token Waste Cost

Interrupted AI workflows lose cached context. The model must rebuild its understanding from scratch. At the Opus tier, the waste factor is 2.8× — meaning for every euro of productive token spend lost, the rebuild costs nearly three. With frequent interruptions, context-rebuild waste can exceed the steady-state token budget.

3. Momentum Loss Cost

Flow state is not a metaphor — it is throughput. During recovery from an interruption, teams operate at 35% reduced velocity (sprint changes) to 60% reduced velocity (key-person departures). The gap between potential and actual throughput is pure opportunity cost.

4. Recruitment Cost (Key-Person Departure)

When a key person leaves, six months of recruitment cost enters the equation — job postings, interviews, onboarding, the slow ramp to productivity. No per-token or per-seat metric captures this. The deployer’s annual envelope absorbs it silently.

5. Sprint Drag (Key-Person Departure)

The remaining team compensates for missing expertise at 25% reduced efficiency for four months. This is the distributed cost of knowledge loss — every sprint runs slower because tacit knowledge left with the person.

The Superlinearity Finding

The five-driver model reveals a non-obvious relationship: interruption cost is superlinear in model tier and sublinear in team size.

Moving from Sonnet to Opus triples the token-waste component because higher-tier models have larger context windows and more expensive cache rebuilds. But doubling the team size less than doubles the human ramp-up cost because coordination overhead is already priced into the team’s baseline.

This has a direct implication for enterprise cost planning: teams running on expensive frontier models are disproportionately exposed to interruption cost. A single CTO departure in a 50-person Opus-tier team can wipe out months of token-cost savings.

The Owned-Compute Advantage

This is where our cost-surface discipline (the CSB-track from our AI Pricing Capitulation analysis) intersects with interruption economics:

Owned-compute architectures reduce the token-waste component of interruption cost because cached context on owned infrastructure has deterministic lifetime — not the variable eviction policies of metered-API providers.

On a metered API, your cached context competes with every other customer for cache slots. When the provider evicts your cache, you pay the full rebuild cost. On owned EU-sovereign compute (our DGX Spark / Jetson / Acer GN100 stack), the cache lifetime is under your control. The interruption cost of the token-waste component drops to near-zero for planned pauses and stays predictable for unplanned ones.
