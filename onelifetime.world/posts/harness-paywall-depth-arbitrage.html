The Harness Paywall

30 July 2026 · Risto Anton Päärni · Espoo, Finland

Three things were said in the last three weeks. Separately, none of them is news. Chained together, they describe where enterprise AI money is about to move.

Act I — The harness is the durable layer

On Microsoft's Q4 earnings call on 29 July, an analyst asked Satya Nadella how Microsoft benefits when enterprises adopt open models. He did not defend the lab relationship. He described an architecture:

"You've got to keep your harness separate from the model. The harness will ensure that your memory, your context, all of that is external. That means any given model at any given time is swappable."

Models are ephemeral. The scaffolding around them — context routing, tool calling, memory, state loops — is what persists. Jensen Huang had said something adjacent on 8 July: most companies will be built on harnesses. The implication is directive. Build for the harness.

Act II — The harness is a token monster

Then Composio published a benchmark that puts a price on that advice. Same model, same 28 tasks, only the harness changed. Success rates came out close. Token consumption did not: a spread of up to 30×, medians of 61k, 67k and 340k tokens per task. At $3 per million input tokens, that is roughly $0.22, $0.28 and $2.00 for the same work.

Read it honestly before you use it: Composio makes one of the three harnesses tested, and it placed near the top of its own leaderboard. The task set is not published. One model, one run. Treat the direction as real and the exact multiple as unsettled.

Even so, the conflict with Act I is hard to escape. If the harness is where the value lives, and the harness burns 340k tokens per task, then under metered API pricing the durable layer is unaffordable at scale. The architecture runs into a paywall.

Act III — The same tokens, the opposite sign

The resolution is not a leaner harness. It is a different cost substrate.

On a metered API, 340k tokens are a variable cost that scales with exactly the behaviour Act I tells you to maximise. On hardware you already own, running an architecture that holds throughput as context accumulates, the external per-token bill disappears.

Be careful with that sentence, because the tempting version of it is false. Tokens on your own hardware are not free. You still pay for electricity, for amortising the box, for maintenance, for serving the model, and for whichever bottleneck you reach first. What changes is the shape of the cost: an unpredictable charge that scales with how hard your agents think becomes a bounded one you sized in advance. The 340k tokens stop being an elastic exposure and become an allocation of capacity you have already provisioned.

Two things had to become true for that to work, and both now have. Sub-quadratic attention — hybrid designs that mix state-space layers with attention — makes a large context window economically usable rather than merely advertised. And inference engines have appeared that hold throughput as the context fills, instead of collapsing to single digits once real work accumulates.

Two workloads, two correct answers

This is not an argument that everyone should buy GPUs. The substrate is selected by the shape of the work, and there are two shapes.

Consumer applications. Millions of external users, arriving unpredictably, each sending a short prompt — one or two thousand tokens of context. You need elastic capacity to absorb bursts you cannot forecast. Paying fractions of a cent per token is far cheaper than owning clusters that sit idle off-peak. Metered APIs are not a compromise here; they are the right answer.

Industrial fleets. A known, bounded number of internal agents — ten, twenty specialised software operators — running deep, multi-turn workflows: a compliance audit, a refactor across a codebase, an evidence chain reasoning over a long record. This is where Composio's 340k median lives. Per-token billing on that depth is where margin goes to die.

The difference that matters is not size. It is who decides the concurrency. In the first case, the public does. In the second, you do.

The part most people will skip

"Local compute is free" is false, and a claim that big deserves its limit stated plainly.

Every GPU has a throughput knee. To the left of it, memory bandwidth and KV-cache comfortably carry the active context and generation stays responsive. Past it, VRAM fills, requests queue, and throughput collapses non-linearly into latency. So the shift does not remove cost — it changes the kind of cost you pay:

  • Metered cloud: cost scales with tokens; concurrency is effectively unlimited. You trade capital for capacity.
  • Own hardware: no external per-token bill; the residual cost is bounded and mostly fixed — power, amortisation, maintenance, serving — and concurrency is capped. You trade peak scale for predictable, dimensioned execution at depth.

Run a consumer app on local GPUs and an unplanned surge pushes you past the knee and takes your performance with it. Run a bounded fleet and you can dimension the engine so that even at 250k–340k context depth, the total workload sits to the left of it. That is the whole trick, and it is a discipline rather than a property: you can only dimension to the left of a knee you have measured. Under-dimension a sovereign deployment and you hit the same wall as everyone else.

We have not yet measured our own. The sweep that would settle it is specific — at what context length does KV-cache bandwidth drag generation below interactive speed, and where does memory-bound generation give way to queueing — and it has to report six things per operating point, not one throughput number: tokens per task, latency, concurrent jobs, GPU memory, queue time, and cost per audit-ready output. That last one is what turns a systems benchmark into a commercial claim, because it is the unit an industrial buyer actually purchases.

Until those exist for our own hardware, everything above is an architectural hypothesis rather than a proven result, and we would rather say so than let it be read as one.

What it means if you are buying

Check the harness before you switch the model. If your agent bill is uncomfortable, harness overhead is now a more likely culprit than model price, and it is the cheaper thing to change.

Harness ownership is now a decision you have to make. As of 29 July, "we keep the harness separate from the model" is an earnings-call talking point from the largest enterprise software vendor on earth — so as a vendor claim it is table stakes, and anyone selling you that sentence alone is describing the floor. The consequential version of it is not a vendor's to answer. If the harness is the durable layer — if it holds your memory, your context, your traces, your accumulated operating knowledge — then whether you own that layer or rent it is a decision every enterprise now has to take deliberately, the way it once decided whether to own its data centre or its ERP. Deferring it is also a decision. It just gets made by whoever is holding your context when you want to change something.

What is defensible is the coupling. Deep scaffolding is only affordable on compute whose cost is bounded rather than metered, and a vendor whose revenue scales with tokens has little reason to make token-intensive work cheap. But be precise about the limit of that argument, because a good procurement team will be: metered vendors can reprice — reserved capacity and provisioned throughput already do. A capacity contract answers the bill. It does not answer where your evidence lives, under whose law, or who can compel its disclosure. Compute makes deep harness work affordable; jurisdiction makes it defensible. The moat is both, and neither one alone.

The harness is the future of software. Its token intensity makes metered pricing the wrong place to run it for a whole class of work. That is the argument, and the interesting job now is finding out precisely where it stops being true.
