(LSJ) Federal Government Regulation of AI

Published on January 21, 2020

excerpt from Joel Nantais entry on Medium. Read the entry here.

Regulation of Artificial Intelligence: The Americas and the Caribbean

What are they proposing to be the most important considerations of AI rules?

  1. Public Trust in AI. Any government regulation of public sector AI must encourage “reliable, robust, and trustworthy AI” and these standards should be the overarching guiding theme.

  2. Public Participation. Agencies should leverage existing mechanisms and requirements to allow for public oversight and participation in any AI regulations or rules. The hope here, as underpins much of the APA, is that the public will voice concerns and share information not considered internally by the government agencies that will reshape the regulations.

  3. Scientific Integrity and Information Quality. The draft rules require agencies to engage with, and rely on, quality scientific research and information when designing regulations on AI. It also requires transparency of this research to allow for updated or contradictory information to be presented. The gets to the heart of the requirements for validity standards for AI models.

  4. Risk Assessment and Management. The draft rules require agencies to approach AI regulations from a risk-based approach. This acknowledges there will be imperfection in AI, but allows for context-based risk assessments in regulation. Therefore, some risks are acceptable if they allow AI to progress in line with the overarching goals, but AI which pose “unacceptable harm, or harm with greater harm than benefits” can be regulated.

  5. Benefits and Costs. A long theme in regulation requires agencies to only pursue rules if the intended benefits of the regulations outweigh the costs of them. This must be true in AI regulations according to the draft rules.

  6. Flexibility. The draft rules require that agencies regulate AI in a way that is flexible to accommodate rapid growth and changes in AI. This should be accomplished by focusing on performance-based regulation, rather than design-based. This means that rules will not dictate “how”, but rather will focus on the “what” of AI.

  7. Fairness and Non-Discrimination. Importantly, the focus of regulations in this area highlights the need for agency regulations to ensure fairness and non-discrimination in accordance with existing laws and rules only. Additionally, the focus should be on the outcomes of the AI decisions and and unintended discrimination compared to existing processes as a baseline.

  8. Disclosure and Transparency. The requirements for regulations of transparency under the draft must be “context based”. The context must consider potential harms, the magnitude of those harms, technical state of the AI, and the potential benefits of the AI. This is an interesting assessment for regulations as it allows for a number of subjective determinations for agencies to consider when determining whether or not o require transparency of AI in the private sector.

  9. Safety and Security. Under this section, agency regulations must focus on the controls over confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the data used in an AI application. The goal is to ensure private sector resilience and protection against attack and exploitation by bad actors.

  10. Interagency Coordination. And the final requirement of the OMB draft rules is to ensure sharing and commonalities between agencies’ regulations. This will be enforced through interagency review of draft regulations before they are made publicly available.
